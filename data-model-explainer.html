How the map reads the forest

Every score is a model, not a measurement. This page explains which layers we actually observe, which we infer, where the gaps are, and what we do about them. No numbers are invented to fill a blank.

What sits under a score

A zone score mixes layers with very different quality. Some are measured at metre resolution, some are inferred from a national map, and some are a documented assumption. We label them so you can judge the answer, and we show a confidence label next to every zone.

  • MeasuredTerrain and canopy. Altitude, slope, aspect, cold-air pooling and canopy cover from ICGC LiDAR at 5 m and 1 m. The most reliable inputs we have.
  • ModelledWeather. A forecast on an ~11 km grid, corrected for the zone's altitude. Useful, but it is a model of the atmosphere, not a gauge in the stand.
  • MappedSoil and habitat. Geology from 1:1,000,000 maps and forest type from CORINE at 100 m. Right at comarca scale, blind below it.
  • AssumedWeights and rules. Rain 35%, temperature 25%, the habitat multipliers, the phenology gate. Chosen from documented field knowledge; not yet fitted to enough outcomes.

Why rain is the hard part

Rain is 35% of the weather score, the single heaviest trigger. The app asks one question: how many millimetres fell in the last 5–10 days, depending on the species, compared with what that species needs.

The number usually comes from one weather grid cell roughly 11 km across, or from a handful of official rain gauges interpolated between. That is a wide brush. A convective autumn storm can drop 40 mm on one side of a massís and 5 mm on the other, eight kilometres away. The nearest gauge often sits in the valley, under-recording what falls on the slope. Two stands 500 m apart get the same rain on paper even when their real totals differ.

It is not an error that grows with the number of days. A longer window does not add spatial error by itself. The problem is that the whole total is sampled at a coarse place. A small spatial offset can decide whether a storm's contribution is in the window or out of it, and that can flip a score.

What we do about it

  • Altitude correction. Temperature is corrected for the zone's height. Rain is not yet, which is an honest gap.
  • Optional official stations. With a free Meteocat key, local XEMA observations replace the modelled rain and are blended into the water balance. The distance and age of the nearest station are shown.
  • Soil water balance. Instead of only summing rain, we track rain minus evaporation over the season, so a wet week after a dry month is not treated the same as a wet week in an already wet month.
  • Radar, planned. A radar composite would give areal rain rather than a point. There is no clean free Catalan radar API today, so this is a later, larger step.

Soil, canopy and confidence

Soil. The 1:1,000,000 geology map separates the limestone Pyrenees from the siliceous zones. It cannot see a local pocket. About a sixth of zones still land in a “mixed” class — our way of saying the map does not know. The finer IGME MAGNA 50 (1:50,000) answers for 49 zones, and the ICGC MSC25M 1:25,000 soil map for 7; the rest keep 1:1M with the reason recorded.

pH. SoilGrids gives a pH estimate at 250 m. It is a global model, slightly high for the Pyrenees, and it times out on many requests. Today it is missing on 45 of 135 zones. We could retry and persist it to reach 135/135, but completeness is not accuracy: a value from a 250 m model is still an estimate, and we prefer a labelled blank to a confident wrong number.

Canopy. LiDAR gives real canopy cover and height. It cannot see undergrowth beneath a closed canopy, so we call our understory value a proxy and show its sample coverage.

Confidence. Every zone carries a confidence label built from data presence, source resolution, station distance and age. It is shown, never multiplied into the prediction. The curated part is derived per zone from that evidence (0.62–0.80; 82 alta / 53 medium), so it ranks zones instead of repeating one value.

The secrecy problem

Improving the weights needs outcomes: where someone looked, and whether they found the mushroom. Those labels are scarce for a social reason, not a technical one. Foragers rarely publish a forest, sometimes name a comarca, and almost never report a failed trip. A model tuned only on public successes learns where people walk, not where mushrooms grow.

So we split the question in two.

Can check now

Discrimination

Using public occurrence records, we ask whether known sites score higher than random points in Catalonia. This is real and already published per species (AUC from about 0.32 to 0.75). It tells us the model separates good from poor places. It cannot tell us the probability of finding something.

Needs your data

Calibration

Turning a score into “roughly how often does this work?” needs found / not-found reports, including the empty trips. With zero reports we publish zero and do not retrain. With enough private, rounded reports we can fit and validate weights honestly.

This is why the app asks for a 30-second report and rounds the location to 1 km. Your exact spot stays private; the pattern becomes evidence. A failed trip is as valuable as a basket.

The six open gaps

These are the data-model tasks we know about. Each one is a trade-off, not a bug.

01 · Rain

The gauge gap

ProblemRain comes from an ~11 km cell or a valley gauge; storms are local.

PlanBlend a radar composite once a usable source exists, adjusted with gauges. Until then, show distance and age, and keep the XEMA option visible.

02 · Soil

The 1:1M ceiling

ProblemA siliceous pocket inside limestone is invisible, and “mixed” flattens the strongest habitat signal.

PlanQuery IGME MAGNA 50 (1:50,000) where the sheet exists, normalise its legend, and keep 1:1M with a recorded fallback where it does not. Shipped 2026-11-15: MAGNA 50 answers for 49 zones and MSC25M 1:25,000 for 7; the CHC3 1:25k habitat is recorded as a shadow block for 50 zones.

03 · pH

Missing pH

ProblemSoilGrids times out on 61 of 135 zones and its 250 m model is biased high in the Pyrenees.

PlanRetry and persist the requests to approach 135/135, label the source and resolution, and never treat completeness as accuracy.

04 · Habitat

The tree species gap

ProblemCORINE knows “conifer”, not Scots pine; the intended 1:25k habitat map is offline, and GeoVeg has been down for a long time.

PlanKeep retrying the official layers, prepare a dormant GeoVeg parser, and let verified manual field observations fill specific stands with a date and source.

05 · Labels

The secrecy gap

ProblemFew public outcomes, and successes are over-reported; locations are secret by culture.

PlanPrivate 1 km reports, a personal logbook, comarca-level signals, and backtests that borrow strength across species. No forced disclosure.

06 · Confidence

The flat label

ProblemThe curated confidence is the same on every zone, and several quality inputs are constants.

PlanDerive confidence from evidence — sources present, resolution, pH, access, gate passed — so the label ranks zones and explains itself.

How you can help

  • Report the empty trips. “Four hours, nothing” is the label the model needs most.
  • Keep the location coarse. The 1 km cell is enough to calibrate; your forest stays yours.
  • Add a field note to a zone. Tree species, undergrowth, soil, water, a washed-out track. A dated observation with a source beats a guess from a national map.
  • Say the comarca, not the spot. If you only want to share “rovellons in the Berguedà this week”, that still helps the regional signal.

What we will not do

  • Invent a coordinate, a pH or a source. A missing value is shown as missing.
  • Hide uncertainty behind one number. The score is broken into habitat, microclimate, weather, season and confidence.
  • Publish your exact spot. Reports are rounded to 1 km; exact locations are private by design.
  • Claim precision we do not have. A zone is a stand-level estimate, not a tree-by-tree answer.
  • Pretend the model is calibrated. With no field reports, AUC and Brier are published as unavailable, not as zero or as a success.

Bolets is a free mushroom-hunting forecast for Catalonia. This page describes the current data model after Phases 1–6; it is an explainer, not a promise of accuracy. The score is a guide for where to look, never a guarantee of finding, and never a substitute for identifying what you pick.

Status snapshot: 46 stations, 135 curated zones, 13 species, LiDAR terrain and canopy on every zone, field reports still at zero.
